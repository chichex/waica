# The perspective camera orbits a followed entity, and the mouse is an Action source

A 3D scene's perspective `camera` block may name an entity to `follow` (issue #181). It then becomes the **Orbit Camera**: it looks at the followed entity's position plus `lookHeight`, from `distance` away at a `yaw` and `pitch` in degrees (yaw 0 looks toward −z, 90 toward +x; pitch is the camera's height above the look point, positive looking down), and it moves once per Simulation Step, from the scene camera step, never per render frame. The block's other orbit fields are the input: the step's locked mouse movement times `sensitivity` turns yaw and pitch (`invertY` flips the vertical), four orbit Actions turn it at `orbitSpeed` degrees per second times their value, pitch is clamped to `minPitch`..`maxPitch`, and the wheel moves the chosen `distance` within `minDistance`..`maxDistance`. With no input the orbit holds: it does not swing back behind the entity. Each step a ray from the look point toward the camera, skipping the followed entity's colliders (sensors never block a ray), places the camera 0.2 in front of what it hits and never closer than 0.5; the chosen distance does not change, so the camera springs back once the wall is gone. A block without `follow` is placed once from `position` and `target`, as before. `game.cameraYaw` reports the orbit's yaw, which a camera-relative mover (`CharacterMotor.relativeTo: 'camera'`) turns its input by; the Runtime Snapshot's `view` adds `follow`, `yaw`, `pitch`, `distance` and `distanceActual`.

The mouse is one more source of the same Actions (ADR 0023). A binding names `Mouse:Left` or `Mouse:Right` beside key codes and `Gamepad:` codes; a press or release of that button on the game canvas drives the Action's held, just-pressed and value exactly as a key does, and a click elsewhere on the page never does. The Input also reports the mouse movement of each Simulation Step while the pointer is locked to the canvas, and the wheel over the canvas, whose page scroll it prevents only there; both are frozen at the step's start beside the gamepad poll and read zero between steps. `game.input.requestPointerLock()` and `game.input.pointerLocked` reach the Pointer Lock API; while an orbit camera plays (the Game simulates), a click on the canvas takes the lock and reaches no Action, and a disposed Game releases a lock it holds. The Runtime Bridge's `look { dx, dy }` adds movement to the next step as if the pointer were locked, so a Run Session orbits without one.

Camera Effects' shake now moves a perspective camera too, along its own right and up axes, by the offset scaled to world units at the look point's depth (twice the distance to it times the tangent of half the field of view, over the Game's view height): the same `shake` call moves the image by the same number of pixels as it does in 2D.

## Considered Options

An orbit expressed as a spherical `offset` vector, or in radians, was rejected for degrees and named angles, which the inspector and the MCP can show as they are. Keeping the orbit inside `game.ts` was rejected by its line budget: `OrbitCamera` (`orbit-camera.ts`) is stepped from `CameraRig.step`, which also keeps the orthographic follow. Mouse buttons listened for on the window, like keys, were rejected: a click on the editor's panels would press `Mouse:Left`. A sphere cast for the camera's collision was not needed: a ray with a margin keeps the camera in front of a wall at the distances an orbit uses. A shake expressed as a yaw/pitch jitter in degrees was rejected because its pixel amplitude would depend on the distance to the look point, unlike the 2D shake.

## Consequences

ADR 0027's fixed perspective camera stands for a block without `follow`; `follow` leaves the orthographic-only fields, while `zoom`, the deadzone, lookahead, smoothing and limits stay orthographic. The engine asks for the pointer lock only on a click while an orbit camera plays, so the editor's edit mode (whose Game does not simulate) never locks, and Esc releases it as the browser always does. The orbit's one-step latency is inherent: a mover reads the yaw the camera had at the start of the step. Smoothing, lock-on and strafing are not designed here.
